The Operable Ledger

The Clinical Ledger records what a receptor's excess or deficit looks like at the bedside. This version asks what puts a patient on one side of that line: which way the drug pushes, how much of the receptor it holds, how long it has been there, and how the brain has adapted. ROOM IIntrinsic activity

Direction depends on what was already there

Whether a drug produces excess or deficit is not a property of the drug alone. It is the drug's own efficacy weighed against the activation the receptor already had, in proportion to how many receptors the drug holds. An antagonist has nothing to remove when tone is low. A partial agonist lowers signaling when tone is high and raises it when tone is low. A dependent patient's set point has moved, so the same dose that intoxicates a naive patient barely registers.

Net signal = (1 − occupancy) × existing activation + occupancy × the drug's efficacy, judged against the patient's set point (steady band ±0.3). Efficacy values are placed so each drug class lands in a recognisable direction; they are not measured intrinsic activities. For GABA-A the stops are allosteric modulators at the benzodiazepine site, so "antagonist" means flumazenil, which returns the receptor to GABA-only signaling.

ROOM IID2 occupancy

One receptor, three thresholds

Kapur and colleagues randomised 22 patients with first-episode schizophrenia to low-dose haloperidol and measured striatal D2 occupancy with [¹¹C]raclopride PET. Occupancy ranged from 38% to 87%. The likelihood of clinical response, hyperprolactinemia and extrapyramidal symptoms rose significantly once occupancy passed 65%, 72% and 78%. The therapeutic window is the narrow strip between the first and the last.

ROOM IIIDopamine pathways

Same receptor, four presentations

A D2 antagonist cannot choose where it binds. The antipsychotic effect in the mesolimbic pathway arrives together with motor effects in the nigrostriatal pathway and prolactin release from the pituitary. Raising dopamine produces the mirror image. Release continues in every mode below; what changes is what the target receptor does with it.

      ROOM VToxidromes

      Tell the syndromes apart at the bedside

      Excess and deficit states are easiest to recognise in their extreme forms. Several look alike from the doorway: hot, fast and agitated. The discriminators are in the eyes, the skin, the bowel sounds, the muscle tone and the clock.

      Hunter serotonin toxicity criteria

      Dunkley 2003

      In 2,222 serotonergic overdoses, five findings (clonus, agitation, diaphoresis, tremor, hyperreflexia) predicted a toxicologist's diagnosis; hypertonia and temperature above 38 °C were added for life-threatening cases. Sensitivity 84% and specificity 97%, against 75% and 96% for Sternbach's criteria.

      ROOM VIIAdaptation

      Stopping a drug flips the sign

      A brain exposed to a drug for weeks adjusts its own responsiveness to bring signaling back toward the set point. That adjustment is tolerance while the drug is present. When the drug is removed the adjustment is still there, and the patient swings to the opposite state: deficit after a chronic agonist, excess after a chronic antagonist.
