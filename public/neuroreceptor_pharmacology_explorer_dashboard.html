      Agent × target affinity
      Affinity is shown as pKi (−log10 of the molar Ki; higher binds tighter, each +1 is 10×). Every value comes from a single source (the NIMH PDSP Ki Database, human receptors only, taken as the median of all human values for that pair), so affinities are comparable across drugs and receptors. Agonist / antagonist direction is curated separately by IUPHAR/BPS, and is shown only where IUPHAR reports it. A ringed dot means the pair was screened and showed no meaningful binding (pKi ≤ 5); a blank cell means PDSP has no human value, which is not the same as no interaction. Affinity is not occupancy. pKi describes how tightly a drug binds in vitro at equilibrium, not how much of the receptor it occupies in a patient: that depends on dose, unbound brain concentration, metabolites, and endogenous tone, none of which are shown here.

      Pharmacology Primer · Agonism & Antagonism

      A drug’s effect at a target is set by two separable properties: affinity (how tightly it binds) and efficacy (what it does once bound). The agonist–antagonist spectrum organises every agent in this cabinet. Definitions below follow contemporary receptor theory (Berg & Clarke, 2018).

      Affinity vs efficacy foundational

      Affinity is the strength of binding (indexed by Kᵢ / pKᵢ, see the Catalogue’s Binding Affinity Plate). Efficacy (intrinsic activity) is the magnitude of response the bound drug produces. The two are independent: an antagonist can bind with very high affinity yet have zero efficacy. Affinity decides where a drug acts; efficacy decides what it does there.

      Full agonist activation

      Binds and produces the maximal receptor response the system can give (high intrinsic efficacy). Increasing dose moves the tissue toward its ceiling.

      Examples here: morphine and methadone at μ-opioid; bromocriptine at D2; epinephrine at β1.

      Partial agonist activation

      Binds and activates, but produces a sub-maximal response even at full occupancy. Because it occupies the receptor while delivering less signal, it behaves as a functional agonist when endogenous tone is low and a functional antagonist when tone is high, a built-in stabiliser with a ceiling effect.

      Examples here: aripiprazole at D2 (Wichniak et al., 2021); buprenorphine at μ-opioid (ceiling on respiratory depression).

      Neutral (silent) antagonist blockade

      Binds with affinity but zero intrinsic efficacy. Alone it does nothing visible; its job is to occupy the site and block agonists. Its clinical effect is therefore tone-dependent, dramatic when an agonist or high endogenous tone is present, silent otherwise.

      Examples here: naloxone and naltrexone at μ-opioid; prazosin at α1; yohimbine at α2.

      Inverse agonist blockade

      Some receptors are active without a ligand (constitutive activity). An inverse agonist binds and reduces that baseline activity, producing the opposite effect of an agonist. It does more than a neutral antagonist, which only blocks.

      Examples here: pimavanserin at 5-HT2A; many β-blockers (e.g. propranolol) act as β1 inverse agonists.

      Competitive vs noncompetitive antagonism blockade

      Competitive antagonists vie for the same (orthosteric) site as the agonist and can be surmounted by raising agonist concentration (e.g. naloxone). Noncompetitive antagonists bind elsewhere, or, for ion channels, inside the open pore, and are not overcome by more agonist.

      Examples here: ketamine, esketamine and memantine are noncompetitive open-channel blockers at the NMDA pore (Hansen et al., 2018).

      Allosteric modulation (PAM / NAM) modulation

      An allosteric modulator binds a site distinct from the agonist and tunes the response up (positive, PAM) or down (negative, NAM) without activating the receptor itself. Because it amplifies the endogenous signal, a PAM needs the natural ligand to be present.

      Examples here: benzodiazepines and Z-drugs are PAMs at the GABA-A benzodiazepine site; barbiturates modulate a separate site (Ghit et al., 2021).

      Tone-dependence principle

      An antagonist or inverse agonist only reveals an effect where there is endogenous agonist tone or constitutive activity. The same drug looks inert in one setting and powerful in another, which is why this cabinet shows agonism and antagonism relative to a physiologic-tone reference, not as fixed “on/off” states.
